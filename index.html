    About Kokoy

    The Language Gap

    When the British took over Dominica from the French, they could not communicate with the enslaved people, who spoke French Creole (Kwéyòl). To bridge that gap, the British brought English-speaking enslaved people from Antigua, Montserrat, and St. Kitts to work specific estates, including Hatton Garden and Woodford Hill.

    A New Culture and Religion

    These new arrivals brought their own customs, Protestant/Methodist religion, and English-based language, which strongly shaped the northeastern villages of Marigot, Wesley, Woodford Hill, and Clifton.

    The Birth of Kokoy

    To communicate across the language barrier, an English-based creole called Kokoy was created, related to the creoles spoken in Jamaica and Antigua.

    How Kokoy Is Viewed Today

    Once dismissed as a dialect for the uneducated, Kokoy is now celebrated and widely accepted, with speakers pushing for its recognition as a valid language in its own right.

    The Name "Kokoy"

    Its origin is uncertain. Local lore says people from other villages thought the accent sounded like someone choking on a piece of kokoy (dry coconut meat).

    Why This Project Exists

    To document and preserve Kokoy for future generations, built and maintained by a community member from Wesley, Dominica.
